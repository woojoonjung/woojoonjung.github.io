Notes.

Representation Learning Can machines understand what lives between people?

Where world understanding asks whether machines can interpret the reality around us, human understanding asks whether they can interpret the realities that exist within and between people. Much of what shapes how we think, feel, and communicate is never stated directly: personal memories, routines, preferences, emotional states, cultural references, and the shared context built over time within families, communities, and relationships.

These aspects of human life are not easily captured as a fixed set of semantic labels. They are distributed across behavior, language, history, and relationships—felt rather than fully articulated. I am interested in how AI systems might recognize these implicit patterns.

Representation Learning Does understanding language mean understanding tables?

Working with tables has made me question an assumption that is easy to make about language models: if they learn to understand language from vast amounts of unstructured text, should understanding a table follow naturally? Both prose and tables can be represented as sequences of discrete tokens. Yet interpreting a table requires recovering relationships expressed through rows, columns, headers, and conventions that may never be explained in words.

How these elements are assembled into a context can shape what information a model is able to use. A representation may retain every value while obscuring which header it belongs to, what its units imply, or which comparisons are meaningful. Information can be present without the relationships needed to reason with it being clear. Language competence alone therefore gives us no reason to take reliable tabular understanding for granted. The question is how models can recognize and reason through the structures that make the data meaningful.

This matters because structured data reflect accumulated human practice. What people and organizations choose to record, how they define categories, and which distinctions they preserve can reflect expertise developed over decades. Some of that knowledge is visible in the data’s organization; other assumptions remain implicit in the practices behind it. Understanding structured data can help make these traces of human knowledge usable by AI. It offers a way to connect the values a model reads with the experience, conventions, and judgments needed to interpret them.

Human-AI Interaction Is interaction just a way to access machine intelligence?

I see the translation layer between machine representations and human experience as a starting point for system design. What should be surfaced, what should remain in the background, and how should people be able to respond? These choices shape not only a person’s experience, but also what the system can learn. What information might these exchanges elicit, and what should the system learn from it? Those questions should inform both the learning objectives and the algorithms themselves. I believe systems can have greater impact when learning and interaction are designed together, each helping shape the other from the outset.

Human-AI Interaction Is human augmentation only about helping us know more?

Expanding a person’s ability to communicate is as important as expanding what they know. A system might help someone convey an experience that is difficult to put into words, make their reasoning visible to others, or explore where their interpretations differ. The question is therefore not only what a person can understand with a machine’s help, but what people can express, discuss, and come to understand together. Exploring these possibilities requires building prototypes and designing and testing these experiences with people.

Design How can we ease everyday friction?

I am drawn to the small moments when everyday systems fail to meet people where they are. Through observation, interviews, and surveys, I like uncovering the subtle frustrations that often go unnoticed: a task that feels unnecessarily complicated, information that arrives too late, or a system that asks too much of people in an already demanding moment. From there, I translate those insights into experiences that make things feel clearer, lighter, and more natural. Whether through a product, interface, or service, I am interested in designing systems that respond to real needs rather than abstract assumptions.

Design How can we enrich human experience?

Design can shape how people notice, feel, remember, and connect with the world around them. This means designing not only individual interactions, but also the relationship between them: how sequential moments build into a narrative, how simultaneous sensory cues reinforce one another, and how an experience can create resonance or evoke a meaningful memory. I am interested in using form, material, interaction, and atmosphere to expand and connect our senses, making ordinary moments feel more immersive and meaningful.
